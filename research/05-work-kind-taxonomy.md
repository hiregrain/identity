# Taxonomy Prior Art for `work_kind` and Responsibility Dimensions

## TLDR

- **No existing taxonomy is directly adoptable wholesale.** O*NET is US-only, occupation-grained (not task/work-kind grained), revised on a slow multi-year cadence, and every occupation code eventually gets split/merged/retired — which is exactly the kind of churn a permanent ledger cannot absorb without a versioning discipline. ESCO is EU-only, occupation+skill grained (no clean "kind of work performed" layer either), and young (v1 launched 2017, still stabilizing). ISCO-08/SOC are coarser still — built for national labor statistics, not for describing what a single attestation of work actually was.
- **The closest real analogue to what you want — a small, coarse, cross-vertical set of "kinds of work" that local taxonomies map up into — doesn't exist as a public standard.** It has to be authored. This is a real, non-trivial cost the founders should budget for, not a research task with an existing answer to copy.
- **Recommendation: author a small proprietary `work_kind` vocabulary (probably 30–150 top-level terms, closer to O*NET's "detailed work activity" grain than its "occupation" grain), publish crosswalk tables to O*NET-SOC and ISCO-08/ESCO for external legibility, and adopt BLS's crosswalk discipline for handling revisions** (old codes never get overwritten in stored records; a separate crosswalk table maps old→new, and every version of the vocabulary is itself versioned and retained). This is standard practice in every mature classification system reviewed below (BLS SOC, ESCO, ICD in healthcare) — nobody just edits the taxonomy in place.
- **On responsibility dimensions: SFIA's "levels of responsibility" (autonomy, influence, complexity, plus business skills/behavioural factors) is the closest prior art and is directly transplantable in structure**, though it's IT-sector-specific in content and was built for role descriptions, not for attesting to a specific completed unit of work. Hay/Korn Ferry's three-factor job evaluation (know-how, problem solving, accountability) and Radford/Mercer leveling (scope, impact, autonomy) independently converge on the same 3–5 dimensions, which is reasonably strong triangulating evidence that "ambiguity/autonomy/complexity/impact/scope" is close to a natural basis for this kind of thing — but all three were designed to evaluate *jobs* (a standing role), not *discrete attestable work events*, and all three are proprietary, compensation-purpose instruments, not open specs. The ledger's 7-dimension list (ambiguity, autonomy, customer exposure, team size, machine leverage, budget, P&L) is broader than any single existing framework and will need its own validation, not just adoption.
- **Confidence level:** High confidence on the structural facts about O*NET/ESCO/ISCO/SFIA (multiple corroborating sources, official taxonomy sites). Medium confidence on "which taxonomy employers/ATSs actually use in practice" — the evidence here is thinner and more vendor-marketing-flavored (Lightcast, LinkedIn blog posts) than independent. Low-to-medium confidence on any quantitative claims sourced from vendor blogs (e.g., "22% reduction in time-to-fill") — treat those as unverified marketing figures, not measured facts.

## 1. O*NET-SOC

**Structure — three layers, not two.**
1. **Occupations layer**: the O*NET-SOC taxonomy itself, built on top of the US Bureau of Labor Statistics' Standard Occupational Classification (SOC). The 2019 version (aligned to 2018 SOC) has 1,016 O*NET-SOC occupational titles, of which 923 are actual "data-level" occupations that get survey data collected against them (the rest are placeholders/reserved). [O*NET Resource Center — Taxonomy](https://www.onetcenter.org/taxonomy.html)
2. **Content Model layer** (the actual grain researchers use): within this, O*NET decomposes work into a **Work Activities hierarchy** — roughly 19,000+ task statements linked to 2,000+ "detailed work activities," rolled up into 325 "intermediate work activities," rolled up further into 41 "generalized work activities" (GWAs). This 4-level task hierarchy (task → detailed activity → intermediate activity → generalized activity) is the part structurally closest to what a `work_kind` field needs — it's a task/activity taxonomy independent of any specific job title. [Lightcast — O*NET taxonomy overview](https://docs.lightcast.io/data/docs/us-o-net-soc-occupation-taxonomy-o-net); [O*NET Resource Center — Overview](https://www.onetcenter.org/overview.html)
3. **Descriptor layer**: each occupation is scored on Likert-type scales (0–7 importance/level) across skills, abilities, knowledge areas, work styles, work values, and context variables. This is closer to your "responsibility dimensions" idea than to `work_kind` — it's a per-occupation profile, not a categorical label.

**Governance and update cadence.** DOL-sponsored, run out of the National Center for O*NET Development. Updates happen on an ongoing multi-method schedule (surveys of incumbents and occupational experts, analyst review), with a stated quarterly database release cycle and a "primary" annual update in Q3. [O*NET Resource Center — Data Updates](https://www.onetcenter.org/dataUpdates.html) In practice each individual occupation is only re-surveyed roughly every **~9 years** on a rotating schedule — the database updates quarterly, but any one occupation's underlying data is stale for years at a stretch. [O*NET content model strengths and limitations, Journal for Labour Market Research (2016)](https://labourmarketresearch.springeropen.com/articles/10.1007/s12651-016-0199-8) A 2010 National Academies panel reviewed O*NET in depth (*A Database for a Changing Economy*) — the full report exists but the specific critique text wasn't retrievable in this pass; treat that report as the primary academic critique to pull if a deeper cite is needed. [National Academies Press, ch.1](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/12814/chapter/3)

**Known criticisms** (moderate confidence, cross-corroborated across a peer-reviewed methods paper and secondary sources):
- **Lag**: rotating 9-year resurvey cycle means individual occupation data can be badly out of date relative to fast-moving work (this is the single most load-bearing criticism for your use case — datacenter technician work, game dev, customer-implementation work are all fast-moving).
- **US-centrism**: built entirely around the US SOC; not designed for or validated against non-US labor markets (directly relevant to your Philippine-ops-support vertical).
- **Grain mismatch**: 1,016 occupations is too coarse to distinguish many real jobs — each occupation is itself an aggregation of multiple distinct jobs, which "obscures differences between individual jobs." [Journal for Labour Market Research (2016)](https://labourmarketresearch.springeropen.com/articles/10.1007/s12651-016-0199-8)
- **Task-level sparsity**: some skills are backed by as few as 3 example tasks vs. 10+ for others, which weakens any downstream inference built on the task layer.
- **New/emerging work**: expert-survey-based classification structurally lags novel occupations (gig work, new tech roles) — confirmed by the content-model methods paper; I could not find a strong independent academic source specifically on gig-economy occupational coverage gaps in this pass (searches returned general gig-economy labor politics, not O*NET-specific critique — flagging this as a weaker-evidence claim).

**Bottom line for your design**: O*NET's *task/activity* layer (not its occupation layer) is the right structural analogue for `work_kind`, but its cadence and US-centrism make it unsuitable as the ledger's primary source of truth — useful as a crosswalk target, not a foundation.

## 2. ESCO (EU)

**Structure — two pillars, ISCO-08 as the occupations spine.**
- **Occupations pillar**: hierarchical, and *explicitly not self-contained at the top* — ISCO-08 supplies the top four levels (major/sub-major/minor/unit group), and ESCO only adds a 5th, finer level of ~2,900+ named occupations underneath. This is a genuinely useful governance pattern: ESCO didn't reinvent the top of the hierarchy, it federated onto an existing international standard and only added local grain at the bottom. [ESCO — Occupations pillar](https://esco.ec.europa.eu/en/about-esco/escopedia/escopedia/occupations-pillar); [ESCO — two-pillar structure](https://esco.ec.europa.eu/en/about-esco/escopedia/escopedia/two-pillar-structure-esco)
- **Skills & competences pillar**: ~13,500 concepts (knowledge, skills, competences bundled together — ESCO deliberately does not separate these three the way some US taxonomies do), organized in its own internal hierarchy with sub-classifications. [ESCO — Skills pillar](https://esco.ec.europa.eu/en/about-esco/escopedia/escopedia/skills-pillar)
- **Relationship layer**: each occupation links to skills as "essential" (required regardless of context) or "optional" (context/employer/country-dependent) — a relevance-weighted many-to-many mapping rather than a strict is-a hierarchy. This "essential vs. optional" pattern is worth stealing for how `work_kind` attaches to local fine-grained taxonomy terms.

**Multilinguality**: full translation into all EU official languages plus Icelandic, Norwegian, and Ukrainian — this is the single area where ESCO is unambiguously more advanced than O*NET, because it was built multilingual from the start rather than translated after the fact. Relevant if the ledger expects Philippine-ops or other non-English-first verticals. [ESCO — ISCO](https://esco.ec.europa.eu/en/about-esco/escopedia/escopedia/international-standard-classification-occupations-isco)

**Crosswalks**: ESCO maintains an official ESCO↔O*NET technical crosswalk (published as a technical report) and inherits its ISCO-08 mapping by construction (each ESCO occupation maps to exactly one ISCO-08 unit group). [ESCO–O*NET crosswalk technical report](https://esco.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2022-12/ONET%20ESCO%20Technical%20Report.pdf)

**Versioning**: ESCO ships explicit version numbers (v1, v1.1.1, v1.2.1, etc.) with documented per-version changes — e.g., v1.1.1 specifically improved the occupation→ISCO-08 mapping. This is a good precedent for "the taxonomy itself is versioned and the version is part of the record," which is exactly the discipline the ledger needs. [ESCO v1.1.1 changelog](https://esco.ec.europa.eu/en/about-esco/escopedia/escopedia/esco-v111)

**Adoption reality**: I could not find strong independent evidence of ESCO's real-world adoption depth outside EU public employment services and EU-funded research tooling (e.g., ML-assisted mapping papers). Treat "ESCO is well-adopted" as **unverified** — the search results show EU institutional usage and academic tooling around it, not evidence of broad private-employer adoption comparable to what LinkedIn or Lightcast claim for their own taxonomies.

## 3. Other vocabularies and real-world employer/ATS usage

**ISCO-08**: 4-level (10 major → 43 sub-major → 130 minor → 436 unit groups), grouped by *skill level and skill specialization*, maintained by the ILO. [ISCO-08 structure](https://isco.ilo.org/en/isco-08). This is a labor-statistics classification, not a work-description taxonomy — too coarse (436 unit groups covering "all jobs in the world") to serve as `work_kind` grain, but it's the right layer to crosswalk *up* into for cross-country statistical comparability.

**SOC (US)**: revised on a fixed **10-year cycle**, deliberately timed to follow NAICS industry-classification revisions (which land in years ending in 2 and 7) so that occupation and industry data update in sync rather than drifting independently. [BLS — Classifications and Crosswalks](https://www.bls.gov/emp/documentation/crosswalks.htm) This "sync revision cycles with a related but separate classification system" pattern is worth noting even though it's not directly applicable.

**Lightcast Open Skills Taxonomy**: the closest thing to a living, commercially-used, cross-vertical skills vocabulary. 3-tier hierarchy (Category → Subcategory → Skill), ~31 top-level Skill Categories, 32,000+ individual skills, mined from hundreds of millions of job postings/resumes/profiles, and — notably — **updated every two weeks with a public changelog**, a cadence an order of magnitude faster than O*NET or ESCO. [Lightcast Open Skills Taxonomy](https://lightcast.io/open-skills); [Lightcast changelog](https://docs.lightcast.dev/updates/skills-taxonomy-changelog) This is real prior art for "how do you keep a taxonomy current without breaking every downstream consumer every two weeks" — worth a deeper look at their changelog mechanics specifically, which this pass didn't have time to dig into.

**LinkedIn Skills Graph**: ~39,000 skills across 26 languages, 374,000+ aliases, 200,000+ inter-skill links, built via a human-in-the-loop pipeline (ML proposes, taxonomists curate) rather than pure top-down design. [LinkedIn Engineering — Building the Skills Graph](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/skills-graph/building-linkedin-s-skills-graph-to-power-a-skills-first-world) This is the strongest real-world precedent for "a graph of skills with aliasing, not a strict tree" — useful if `work_kind` terms need aliasing (e.g., "site reliability" vs. "SRE work" vs. "infra ops") more than they need a rigid hierarchy.

**What employers/ATSs actually use**: the evidence here is weak. General secondary sources (ClearCompany, Udemy Business, a WEF adoption toolkit) describe *process* for building an org-specific skills taxonomy and housing it inside an existing ATS, but do not name which external standard (O*NET vs. ESCO vs. Lightcast vs. proprietary) dominates in practice. My honest read: **there is no dominant external standard in ATS practice** — most employers and most ATS vendors either build a proprietary taxonomy or license a commercial one (Lightcast and LinkedIn's data being the two most commercially prominent), and O*NET/ESCO function more as government/labor-market-statistics references than as operational HR-tech standards. Quantitative claims like "cut mis-hires by 90%" and "16x bigger talent pools" appearing in these sources are vendor/advocacy figures, not independently verified — flagging explicitly per your epistemic-honesty standard, do not cite these as fact. [WEF Global Skills Taxonomy Adoption Toolkit 2025](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Global_Skills_Taxonomy_Adoption_Toolkit_2025.pdf)

## 4. Grain, governance, and the "author vs. adopt" question

**How BLS handles SOC revisions without corrupting longitudinal data**: the SOC revises on a fixed 10-year cycle, and BLS publishes formal crosswalk tables between consecutive versions (e.g., 2010 SOC → 2018 SOC) rather than silently remapping historical records. [BLS SOC crosswalks](https://www.bls.gov/soc/soccrosswalks.htm) The crosswalk construction methodology is itself documented: start from the prior crosswalk, examine detailed definitions and example titles to confirm matches, and explicitly decide whether a mapping is one-to-one or one-to-many — with a stated principle of parsimony (don't over-map every incidental overlap). [ISCO-SOC crosswalk process, BLS](https://www.bls.gov/soc/isco_soc_crosswalk_process.pdf) The load-bearing lesson: **old codes are never edited in place**; a historical record keeps the code that was true at the time it was recorded, and a separate, versioned crosswalk table is the only thing that changes. This is the mechanism the ledger needs to copy exactly — an attestation's `work_kind` value should be permanently bound to the taxonomy *version* it was tagged under, with crosswalk tables (not retroactive edits) handling reclassification.

**Author vs. adopt — honest trade-off assessment:**

| | Adopt O*NET/ESCO wholesale | Author a small proprietary vocabulary with crosswalks |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-vertical coverage (datacenter, hospitality, game dev, Philippine ops, customer implementation) | Poor — O*NET is occupation-grained and US-only; ESCO is EU-only; neither vertical fits cleanly into either without heavy re-derivation | Full control — grain chosen to fit exactly the verticals you have |
| External legibility / credibility with employers, regulators, academics | High — "this maps to O*NET code X" is instantly interpretable | Zero on day one; must be earned via published crosswalks |
| Update velocity | Slow (O*NET: ~9yr per-occupation resurvey; ESCO: versioned but not fast) | As fast as you want — but you own the maintenance burden entirely |
| Governance legitimacy (who decides the vocabulary) | Externally validated (DOL, EU Commission/ILO) | You have to build and defend this yourself — this is a real, ongoing cost |
| Risk of grain mismatch corrupting comparisons | High if forced — e.g., "Philippine ops support" has no clean O*NET occupation | Low — grain designed for the actual attestation use case |
| Longitudinal stability for the ledger's own records | Inherits O*NET/ESCO's revision cycle and politics, which you don't control | You control the versioning discipline, but must actually execute it (see BLS pattern above) |

**Recommendation** (stated as recommendation, not settled fact — this is a design judgment call, not something the sources above prove): author a small `work_kind` vocabulary at roughly O*NET's "detailed/intermediate work activity" grain (dozens to low hundreds of terms, not 1,000+ occupations and not a handful of mega-buckets), and publish crosswalk tables to O*NET-SOC and ISCO-08/ESCO for external legibility — mirroring exactly how ESCO federated onto ISCO-08 rather than inventing its own top layer. Adopt BLS's crosswalk discipline (versioned vocabulary, immutable historical bindings, explicit one-to-one/one-to-many crosswalk tables) from day one, because retrofitting it after attestations exist is much harder than building it in.

**Open question this research does not resolve**: who authors the vocabulary and adjudicates disputes as verticals are added (an internal taxonomy team? a rotating panel per vertical, similar to how O*NET uses occupational experts? a public RFC process like some open-source taxonomies use?). None of the sources reviewed here provide a governance model built for a *private, for-profit, cross-vertical* taxonomy — O*NET and ESCO are both government-run public goods, and Lightcast/LinkedIn's governance (internal taxonomist teams + ML) is proprietary and undocumented in enough detail to copy directly. This is a genuine gap the founders need to design themselves, not something prior art hands you.

## 5. Responsibility dimensions — closest prior art

**SFIA (Skills Framework for the Information Age) — most directly on point.**

Structure: SFIA defines ~100+ skills, each assessed against up to 7 **levels of responsibility**: Level 1 Follow, Level 2 Assist, Level 3 Apply, Level 4 Enable, Level 5 Ensure/advise, Level 6 Initiate/influence, Level 7 Set strategy/inspire/mobilise. [SFIA — Levels of responsibility](https://sfia-online.org/en/sfia-9/responsibilities) Each level is defined by five **generic attributes**, which is the part that transplants most directly:
- **Autonomy** — ability to work without close supervision, own outcomes, manage priorities; ranges from "follows clear guidance" at L1 to "sets direction, manages risk, exercises significant independent judgment" at L7.
- **Influence** — scope of impact on others/organization.
- **Complexity** — range and intricacy of tasks and responsibilities.
- **Business skills** and **behavioural factors** — communication, planning, problem-solving effectiveness (these describe *how* the role is performed, not the scope of *what* it covers).
[SFIA — How SFIA works](https://sfia-online.org/en/about-sfia/how-sfia-works); [WithYouWithMe on SFIA generic levels](https://withyouwithme.com/blog/why-sfias-generic-levels-of-responsibility-are-a-powerful-way-to-understand-leadership-and-soft-skills/)

What to borrow: the *structural move* of separating a small set of generic, skill-independent responsibility dimensions from the domain-specific skill/task content, and scoring each dimension on an ordinal scale that's explicitly defined at every level (not just "low/medium/high"). This is precisely the shape of your ambiguity/autonomy/customer-exposure/etc. dimensions sitting alongside vertical-specific `work_kind`.

What to avoid / can't borrow directly: SFIA's levels describe a **standing role or skill proficiency**, assessed by an assessor against a published rubric, not a **discrete completed unit of work** self-attested or counter-attested at the time it happened. SFIA also has only 3 "pure" generic attributes (autonomy, influence, complexity) plus 2 behavioral ones — your 7-dimension list (ambiguity, autonomy, customer exposure, team size, machine leverage, budget, P&L) is both broader and includes dimensions SFIA doesn't have at all (machine leverage, P&L, team size are absent from SFIA; SFIA's "influence" and "complexity" don't cleanly decompose into your 7). Treat SFIA as validating the *shape* of the idea, not the *content* of your specific 7 dimensions.

**Hay/Korn Ferry Guide Chart-Profile Method** — three factors: **Know-How** (depth/breadth of knowledge and skill required), **Problem Solving** (complexity of the challenges the job faces), **Accountability** (freedom to act, and the magnitude/impact of decisions). [Umbrex — Hay Job Evaluation Framework](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/hay-job-evaluation-framework/); [Korn Ferry Hay Guide Chart overview](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/638cdae05d25ea7a456cce1e/t/6718126672f8cd28798f95f3/1729630823433/An+overview+of+Korn+Ferry+Hay+Guide-Chart+ProfileSM+(002).pdf) This is the most widely used job-evaluation methodology globally (per secondary sources — not independently verified here), used for *compensation banding*, i.e., a proprietary, paid, point-factor consulting product, not an open spec you could reference or crosswalk to. Its "Accountability" factor (freedom to act + impact/magnitude of decisions) maps closely onto your "autonomy" + "budget/P&L" dimensions combined.

**Radford (Aon)/Mercer leveling** — job-family leveling systems (Radford: P1–P6 Entry→Principal) scored on **scope, impact, and autonomy**, used heavily in tech/life-sciences compensation benchmarking. [Compensly — Radford levels](https://compensly.com/blog/understanding-job-leveling-radford-levels-and-compensly/) Same caveat as Hay: proprietary, compensation-purpose, standing-role-grained, not open.

**Convergence signal**: three independently-developed frameworks (SFIA from IT/public-sector, Hay from general management consulting, Radford from tech/life-sciences comp) all converge on a small set of dimensions in the same conceptual family — autonomy/freedom-to-act, complexity/problem-solving, and impact/accountability/scope. That's reasonably good triangulating evidence that these three are close to a natural, near-universal basis for "capability grain" across domains. It is **not** evidence that your specific 7-dimension list is correctly specified — none of the three frameworks include machine leverage, customer exposure, or team size as first-class dimensions, and none were built for a per-attestation (rather than per-role) grain. The 7-dimension list should be treated as a hypothesis to pilot-test against real attestations across your four target verticals, not as something prior art has already validated.

## 6. Bottom line: recommended design constraints

1. **`work_kind` should be authored, not adopted wholesale**, at a grain closer to O*NET's "detailed/intermediate work activity" layer (task/activity-level) than its "occupation" layer — occupation-grained taxonomies (O*NET, ESCO, ISCO, SOC) are all too coarse or too US/EU-specific to serve directly as the cross-vertical comparison layer you need.
2. **Publish crosswalk tables to O*NET-SOC and ISCO-08/ESCO from day one**, following ESCO's own pattern of federating onto ISCO-08's top levels rather than reinventing them — this buys external legibility without surrendering control of the grain.
3. **Version the vocabulary explicitly (ESCO-style: v1, v1.1, …) and never edit historical bindings in place.** Every attestation's `work_kind` should be permanently bound to the taxonomy version active when it was recorded; reclassification happens via a separate, versioned crosswalk table, exactly as BLS does for SOC revisions.
4. **Update cadence should be closer to Lightcast's (weeks, with a public changelog) than to O*NET's (per-occupation ~9-year resurvey) or ESCO's (annual-ish major versions)** — a startup adding verticals in real time cannot wait years for its own vocabulary to catch up, but every change still needs to be logged and crosswalked, not silently applied.
5. **Consider a graph-with-aliases structure (LinkedIn Skills Graph pattern) rather than a strict tree** for the local fine-grained vertical taxonomies that translate up into `work_kind` — real-world terms for the same work vary by vertical and region (e.g., "Philippine ops support" vs. a US-labeled equivalent), and a strict single-parent hierarchy will force bad choices that aliasing avoids.
6. **Governance is an unsolved problem you must design, not borrow** — O*NET and ESCO are government-run public goods with legitimacy models (DOL sponsorship, EU Commission mandate) that don't transfer to a private ledger company. Decide explicitly, before scaling past the first few verticals, who has authority to add/split/merge `work_kind` terms and how vertical experts get a voice — an unaddressed version of this problem is the single most likely source of taxonomy rot as verticals multiply.
7. **For responsibility dimensions, borrow SFIA's structural move** (a small number of generic, domain-independent dimensions, each scored on an explicitly-defined ordinal scale, decoupled from the vertical-specific work content) **but do not treat the specific 7-dimension list as validated** — SFIA/Hay/Radford converge on autonomy, complexity, and impact/accountability as near-universal, but none of them include machine leverage, customer exposure, or team-size-directed as first-class dimensions, and all three were built for standing-role evaluation, not per-attestation scoring. Pilot the 7 dimensions against real attestations in at least 2–3 of your target verticals before treating the list as fixed.
8. **Treat "grain mismatch destroys comparability" as the central risk to design against**, not a secondary concern — it's the most consistently cited failure mode across O*NET (occupation aggregation obscures real job differences), ESCO (only 5 hierarchy levels below an international standard not built for EU labor-market specificity), and the general skills-taxonomy literature. The four example verticals in your prompt (datacenter technician, hospitality FOH, game dev, Philippine ops) are different enough in structure that a `work_kind` vocabulary built by generalizing from only one or two of them will likely mis-grain badly for the others — the vocabulary needs deliberate construction across verticals from the start, not extension-by-analogy from an initial vertical.
