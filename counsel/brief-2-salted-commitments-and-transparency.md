# Counsel Engagement Brief 2 — Salted Commitments, Public Transparency Log, and Post-Deletion Anonymization

**Jurisdictions:** European Union (GDPR/EDPB), Philippines (DPA), India (DPDP)
**Date prepared:** 2026-08-17
**Status of this document:** Instructions to counsel. Internal research memos cited are not legal advice and have not been counsel-reviewed; source URLs are provided for independent verification.

---

## 1. Background — what the company and product are

The company operates a **persistent worker identity and work-attestation ledger**: workers hold a permanent platform identity and accumulate cryptographically signed attestations of work performed (engagements, training, credentials), issued by registered attesting parties — initially the company's own operating verticals, later external partner companies. The record is **worker-owned**: two founder rulings fix the posture. **R1:** the platform is the worker's portable verified resume and identity layer (modeled on worker-published-profile platforms), not a reporting agency. **R2:** the worker may **delete their whole profile at any time**, but may never edit an already-verified record while the profile exists. The product's credibility claim — "the party said this, the ledger recorded it, and nothing was altered" — depends on tamper-evidence that survives even the operator's own dishonesty. That is what creates this brief's subject matter.

**The integrity architecture.** All records are split across two planes:

- An **append-only integrity spine**: object IDs, opaque subject/issuer references, timestamps, signature hashes, supersession pointers, and — the object of this brief — a **salted cryptographic commitment** of each attestation payload. Commitments are computed as `HMAC(canonical_payload, k_r)` where `k_r` is a **per-record random key (salt) stored only in the erasable payload plane**. Bare or deterministic hashes are deliberately never used, because low-entropy payloads would be dictionary-attackable and because EDPB guidance treats hashes of personal data as personal data.
- An **erasable payload store** holding the actual content and all PII, per-person envelope-encrypted.

**Tamper-evidence machinery (ratified engineering rulings, 2026-08-17).** From day one, per-stream Merkle roots over the spine are checkpointed every ≤5 minutes, KMS-signed, and written to **compliance-mode WORM object storage** (S3 Object Lock in a segregated account, mirrored to a second cloud) — immutable by construction, but not public. Before the first *external* attesting party is activated (or 1M registered identities, whichever first), the checkpoints migrate into a **public, witnessed transparency log** (Certificate-Transparency-style tiled log): a public checkpoint endpoint, independent co-signing witnesses, and published inclusion proofs. **Publication is irreversible** — once checkpoints are public, every commitment in them is public-by-destiny, forever. The log contains three entry families: attestation commitments (as above), registry/key lifecycle events, and privileged operations — including **deletions as anonymous tombstones** ("person-scope deletion executed at time T", nothing linkable post-shred). Payloads, readable personal data, derived values, and the read log are excluded from the log outright.

**What deletion does (R2).** Whole-profile deletion purges the person's payload plane within a published window and **destroys both the per-person data-encryption keys and every per-record salt `k_r`**. The retained spine/log leaf becomes, on our analysis, an unlinkable random value: without the salt, the commitment cannot be recomputed from a guessed payload, and it links to nothing. The tombstone entry proves timely execution of the deletion without identifying the person.

The engineering courts that ratified this design flagged one thing as expressly **not theirs to decide**: "salted commitments discharge linkability technically; the legal posture is counsel's to bless" — before checkpoints go public, because publication is the moment the posture becomes irreversible. That blessing is this engagement.

## 2. Questions presented

1. **Personal-data status pre-deletion.** While a worker's profile exists (salts intact, payloads live), we treat spine commitments as **personal data** (pseudonymized) and handle them accordingly. Confirm or correct, under (a) GDPR, in light of EDPB Guidelines 02/2025's position that hashed data remain personal where re-identification is reasonably likely, and *EDPS v. SRB*'s recipient-relative test; (b) PH DPA Sec. 3(g)/(i); (c) India DPDP Sec. 2(t).
2. **Anonymization post-deletion.** After R2 deletion — payload destroyed, per-person keys crypto-shredded, per-record salts destroyed — is the retained commitment **anonymized** (no longer personal data) under each regime? Specifically: (a) does keyed-HMAC-then-key-destruction escape the EDPB's "hashes are personal data" position, given that the guidance's stated concern (re-identification reasonably likely) is answered by the destroyed key; (b) does any regulator or authority treat salt/key destruction as *deletion of the derived value itself*, and is there contrary authority; (c) what residual metadata on the spine (timestamps, issuer references, supersession topology around a tombstoned subject ID) could re-cross the identifiability line by inference, and what pruning or coarsening would counsel require?
3. **Immutable public checkpoints.** Is there any objection, in any of the three regimes, to **publishing** signed Merkle checkpoints (and later the full tiled log) that contain such commitments, given that publication is permanent and worldwide? Sub-questions: (a) does public, immutable retention of commitments whose subjects have *not* deleted (i.e., still personal data on our own analysis, per Question 1) require a lawful basis and, if so, is legitimate interest in tamper-evident integrity sufficient; (b) does the permanence of WORM/public storage itself violate storage-limitation principles (GDPR Art. 5(1)(e); PH Sec. 11(e); DPDP Sec. 8(7)) for the pre-deletion period; (c) is the answer different for the private compliance-mode WORM anchors (day one) versus the public witnessed log (later) — i.e., can we treat the private-anchor phase as lower-risk and defer any remediation to the migration?
4. **The deletion-tombstone design.** The log records each deletion as an anonymous tombstone. (a) Is the tombstone itself compliant evidence of erasure (GDPR Art. 5(2) accountability; PH NPC Advisory 2021-01 documentation expectations; DPDP Sec. 8), or does it create a new personal-data item (an event *about* an identifiable person at the moment of writing, before unlinkability takes hold)? (b) Should the tombstone be time-delayed or batched to defeat correlation attacks (matching a deletion tombstone's timestamp against a subject's last-observed activity)?
5. **Erasure completeness.** If a regulator ordered erasure of "all personal data" of a deleted worker, would the retained commitments and tombstones — which we *cannot* remove from WORM storage or a witnessed public log — constitute non-compliance? If the answer is "arguably yes" in any regime, what is the strongest defensible position (technical impossibility plus anonymization-by-key-destruction; Art. 17(3)(b)/(e)-style exemptions; PH Sec. 16(e) "blocking" as the discharged remedy), and what disclosure should the worker-facing deletion notice make about what is retained?
6. **Drafting consequences.** What language must the worker consent/notice instrument and the privacy notice contain about (a) the existence of permanent cryptographic commitments, (b) their claimed anonymity after deletion, and (c) the public log — so that the posture is disclosed rather than discovered? (Drafting itself is Brief 4; here we need the required substance.)

## 3. Relevant facts and our current working analysis

**GDPR/EDPB.** EDPB Guidelines 02/2025 on blockchain (v2.0 adopted 7 July 2026) are the closest authority and cut both ways: they *recommend* our exact architecture (do not write cleartext, encrypted, or hashed personal data to the immutable structure; store personal data off-chain; write only a commitment/reference on-chain; erase by deleting the off-chain payload, "after which the on-chain entry has no personal reference") while also stating that encrypted and hashed data remain personal data where re-identification is reasonably likely (https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2025-04/edpb_guidelines_202502_blockchain_en.pdf; practitioner analysis: https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/netherlands/edpb-adopts-final-guidelines-on-blockchain-and-personal-data-a-practical-guide-for-organisations; CNIL's earlier position: https://www.cnil.fr/en/blockchain-and-gdpr-solutions-responsible-use-blockchain-context-personal-data). Our design difference from the criticized pattern is the keyed commitment: a salted HMAC whose key is destroyed at deletion is not recomputable by anyone, which we read as answering the guidance's re-identification concern. Crypto-shredding generally: accepted in practice by many DPAs, but the EDPB position makes it "strong mitigation, not a blessed substitute" — our research grades this genuinely unsettled, medium confidence (`research/01-regulatory-perimeter.md` §2.2). *EDPS v. SRB* (C-413/23 P, 4 Sept 2025) supports a contextual, recipient-relative identifiability test (https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/talking-tech/en/articles/2025/09/pseudonymized-data-after-edps-v-srb.html; https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/09/analysis-of-the-cjeu-judgment) — helpful for the public log, whose readers hold only unlinkable values.

**Philippines.** The DPA (RA 10173, https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html) and IRR (https://www.officialgazette.gov.ph/2016/08/25/implementing-rules-and-regulations-of-republic-act-no-10173/) contain no anonymization guidance of EDPB depth. Relevant texture: Sec. 16(e) lists **blocking** as an alternative remedy to removal/destruction — our hook for the position that suppressing all readability discharges the right even where a commitment is retained; whether the NPC would accept commitment-retention with payload destroyed as "destruction" is untested (research flag, `research/01` §3.2). Criminal exposure runs to officers personally (Secs. 25–35), which raises the stakes of getting the characterization wrong. The NPC has shown willingness to act against foreign identity infrastructure (Tools for Humanity CDO, Oct 2025: https://privacy.gov.ph/npc-issues-cease-and-desist-order-against-tools-for-humanity/).

**India.** DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025 (notified 13 Nov 2025). Our internal research has *not* analyzed DPDP anonymization doctrine; the Act's definition of personal data and the treatment of anonymized/non-identifiable data should be taken from primary text by counsel. This is the least-developed leg of the brief by design.

**Engineering record.** The commitment scheme, log-entry families, exclusion list, and the R2-compatibility mechanism are specified in `design/stack-litigation/d3-verdict.md` §(e) (consolidated log-contents specification) and ratified in `design/stack-litigation/docket-rulings-0.1.md` (D3; founder-call 5 explicitly reserves "counsel blessing of the salted-commitment posture before checkpoints go public"). The private WORM anchors run from day one; the public witnessed log is deadline-bound to first-external-party activation or 1M identities.

## 4. What the answer will drive, and when it is needed

The answer gates **whether the signed checkpoints may become public** — the registry state machine will not activate the first external attesting party until the witnessed public log is live, and the log cannot responsibly go live without this opinion, because publication is irreversible. Timing: the log build begins immediately post-launch and the external-party deadline is commercially plausible within 2–4 quarters (the founder has ruled the first external party is *not* confirmed within ~2 quarters, so this is not an emergency, but the build clock is running). **Needed: before the transparency log's first public checkpoint — target within two quarters.** Secondary consequences: Question 2(c) may force changes to what spine metadata is written at all (cheap now, impossible after publication); Question 6 feeds the worker-agreement drafting in Brief 4; Question 5's answer feeds the deletion-notice copy and the regulator-response playbook.

## 5. What we are explicitly not asking

- **Not** data residency or cross-border transfer of the spine — Brief 1 (this brief assumes the spine replicates globally and asks only about its content).
- **Not** whether erasure *rights* are triggered or how the whole-profile-vs-line-item deletion model fares — Brief 1, Question 3–4.
- **Not** FCRA/CRA classification (Brief 4), attester liability (Brief 3), or worker-agreement drafting (Brief 4, though Question 6 feeds it).
- **Not** an audit of the cryptography itself (HMAC construction, key-destruction procedure) — engineering-verified separately; counsel may assume the technical claims as stated and should say so if any legal conclusion depends on a technical property we have not stated.
- **Not** US state biometric/privacy statutes — no biometric data is in scope of the commitments.
