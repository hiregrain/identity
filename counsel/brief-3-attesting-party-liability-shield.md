# Counsel engagement brief 3: attesting-party liability shield, contract architecture for honest negative attestations

**Jurisdictions:** United States (multi-state, qualified-privilege and public-policy analysis), Philippines (tort, civil code, criminal libel)
**Date prepared:** 2026-08-17
**Status of this document:** Instructions to counsel. Internal research memos cited are not legal advice and have not been counsel-reviewed; source URLs are provided for independent verification.

---

## 1. What the company and product are

The company operates a **persistent worker identity and work-attestation ledger**. Workers hold a permanent platform identity and accumulate **signed attestations** about work performed, such as engagements, training completions, credentials, and skills demonstrated, issued by registered "attesting parties": initially the company's own operating verticals (e.g., game-development, Philippine ops support, datacenter-technician training), and thereafter external partner companies. The canonical external case: a datacenter company contracts the company to train and supply technicians; all trainees and hires route through the ledger, and the datacenter company both reads workers' records and writes attestations into them.

Two founder rulings fix the posture. **R1: worker-owned platform:** the worker owns the record; workers grant and revoke reader access at the party level; the platform is positioned as the worker's portable verified resume, not a reporting agency. **R2: deletion yes, editing no:** a worker may delete their entire profile at any time, but cannot edit an already-verified record while the profile exists. Attestations are append-only from issuance; the correction path is a superseding attestation from the same issuer, and the dispute path (below) can end in a permanent worker counter-statement, never in silent edits.

**Why this brief exists.** The product's value depends on attestations being *honest and complete*, including negative-but-true ones ("did not complete training," "engagement terminated for cause"). Our adversarial-model research concluded that without a deliberate legal structure, the record will reproduce the known US reference-checking equilibrium: employers, though largely protected by qualified privilege, are risk-averse enough about defamation exposure that the market norm collapsed to "name, rank, dates only," a documented chilling effect that starves the record of signal. The research's constraint 11: *attesting parties need a conditional liability shield or the record goes silent*, and it must exist **before the first external attesting party is activated**. The trust registry is designed not to activate an external party until this structure exists.

**The dispute machinery the shield sits on** (already designed, product not statute): a worker files a structured dispute against a specific attestation; the issuing party is notified and a time-boxed window opens (30 days, +15 on new evidence); outcomes are (a) party agrees → superseding correction, (b) party stands → the worker's **counter-statement attaches permanently and is co-rendered wherever the attestation is surfaced**, (c) party unresponsive → a visible "unresolved dispute, issuer unresponsive" flag. Nothing is edited or deleted by dispute. All reads of a record are logged and worker-visible.

## 2. Questions presented

1. **The conditional shield, US.** We propose a contract structure in which (a) the **worker agreement** contains informed consent to receive attestations from parties the worker engages with, plus a **covenant not to sue attesting parties (and the platform) for attestations made in good faith, without malice, and within the attestation schema**, expressly not covering knowingly false statements, malice, or retaliation for protected activity; and (b) the **party agreement** contains the party's warranty of good-faith, factually-grounded attestation, its submission to the dispute process, and an indemnity to the platform for attestations breaching that warranty. Questions: (i) Is a pre-dispute covenant not to sue for defamation/negligence in this setting enforceable, and in which major states is it void or restricted as against public policy (California, New York, Texas, Illinois at minimum; identify others with categorical rules)? (ii) Can the covenant be structured as consent/waiver that *converts* the common-law qualified privilege into a contractual one (consent as an absolute privilege to defamation), and is that stronger than the covenant framing? (iii) What carve-outs are mandatory for enforceability (statutory retaliation claims, discrimination claims, intentional torts, non-waivable statutory rights)?
2. **Does the architecture itself strengthen or weaken the privilege?** The attestation is (a) requested/consented by the worker in advance, (b) visible to the worker immediately, (c) disclosed only to readers the worker has authorized, (d) disputable through a formal process with a permanent counter-statement. Our working view is that each feature maps onto classic qualified-privilege factors (interest of recipient, limited publication, absence of malice inferable from process). Does counsel agree that this design materially improves the attester's defense posture relative to an unsolicited reference call? And is there any state where worker-authorized publication to multiple future readers *loses* the privilege because publication is broader than a specific hiring inquiry?
3. **Reference-immunity statutes.** Most US states have employer reference-immunity statutes (presumption of good faith for job-performance references). (i) Do these statutes cover structured, signed attestations delivered through a platform rather than a reference given to a prospective employer on request? (ii) Should the party agreement recite reliance on them, and are there states whose statutes are conditioned on formats or notice we should build into the product (e.g., copy-to-worker requirements)?
4. **Platform (republisher) exposure, US.** The platform stores and serves party-authored attestations to worker-authorized readers. (i) Does Section 230(c)(1) protect the platform for party-authored attestation content, given that we impose schema, provenance grading, and dispute machinery but do not author the content? (ii) Where is the line? Do our provenance grades, derived flags ("issuer since suspended," "unresolved dispute"), or any derived standing summary constitute platform-authored content outside 230? (iii) Does the answer change if the platform is later held to be a consumer reporting agency (that question is Brief 4's; here we need only the branch: what does the shield architecture need so it does not depend on the 230 answer)?
5. **Philippines.** *Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. Court of Appeals* (G.R. No. 81262, 25 Aug 1989) grounds tort liability (Civil Code Arts. 19/20/21 abuse of rights; Art. 26 privacy) for oppressive employer conduct including derogatory communications about a worker; there is no Section 230 analogue and no US-style codified reference privilege; **criminal libel** (Revised Penal Code Arts. 353–355) applies, with RA 10175 raising the penalty one degree for online publication. Our research flags that a US-hosted platform publishing employer statements about named Philippine workers should treat criminal libel as first-order risk (secondary-sourced; verify). Questions: (i) What is the actual privilege landscape for employment references in PH doctrine (qualifiedly privileged communications under Art. 354(1)?) and does worker consent plus platform dispute process bring signed attestations inside it? (ii) Can the worker's covenant not to sue operate against Civil Code tort claims, and is it void as against public policy or under labor-protective doctrines? (iii) For criminal libel, where consent of the offended party and privileged-communication doctrine intersect: does the designed structure (worker-solicited, worker-visible, limited-audience, disputable) negate publicity or malice as elements, and what residual criminal exposure remains for attesting employers, platform officers, or both? (iv) DOLE Labor Advisory 06-20 makes the statutory Certificate of Employment deliberately thin (dates, position, see https://dole.gov.ph/news/labor-advisory-no-06-20-guidelines-on-the-payment-of-final-pay-and-issuance-of-certificate-of-emplo/); does a richer attestation instrument face any DOLE-side objection?
6. **The dispute/counter-statement process as mitigation.** Does the formal dispute path, which includes notice, time-boxed investigation, and superseding correction or permanent co-rendered counter-statement, function in either jurisdiction as (i) evidence negating malice, (ii) a mitigation-of-damages fact, or (iii) a basis for requiring exhaustion before suit (can the agreements require the worker to complete the dispute process before litigating)? Should the agreements make the dispute process a contractual condition precedent, and is that enforceable?
7. **Drafting deliverable.** Provide the operative clause set: worker-agreement consent + covenant (with state-law severability/choice-of-law strategy), party-agreement warranty + indemnity + dispute-SLA, and the platform's own terms tying both together. Flag any clause that must vary by state or must not be attempted in PH.

## 3. Relevant facts and our current working analysis

**The chilling equilibrium (US).** Employer references are protected by qualified privilege, under which false statements alone do not create liability absent malice, yet employers systematically adopted "name, rank, dates only" policies out of defamation risk-aversion; HR literature documents this as a broken equilibrium that starves hiring of signal (FindLaw: https://www.findlaw.com/employment/hiring-process/is-a-former-employer-s-bad-reference-illegal.html; Legal Aid at Work: https://legalaidatwork.org/factsheet/workplace-defamation/; ERE, "What's Wrong With Reference Checks": https://ere.net/whats-wrong-with-reference-checks-part-1; Wolters Kluwer: https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/expert-insights/balancing-requirements-and-restrictions-when-providing-employment-references). Retaliatory references are independently unlawful as retaliation, separate from defamation (Justice at Work: https://www.justiceatwork.com/is-defamation-ever-considered-retaliation/). Hence the shield must be *conditional*: good faith in, malice and retaliation out. Our research recommends mirroring the existing doctrine contractually: a shield conditioned on good-faith, non-retaliatory, factually-grounded attestation, paired with a low-friction dispute path so workers do not need litigation to contest a bad attestation (`research/06-adversarial-threat-model.md` §5 and design constraint 6).

**The FCRA-shaped dispute process as prior art.** The dispute mechanism deliberately mirrors the credit-reporting dispute structure (investigation window, right to a permanent statement of dispute, "disputed" distinguished from "removed") as the one mature, litigation-tested precedent for contested third-party statements at scale (CFPB: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-if-i-disagree-with-the-results-of-my-credit-report-dispute-en-1327/; NCLC: https://www.nclc.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/cf_disputing-errors-in-a-credit-report.pdf). It is adopted here as product design under R1, not as statutory compliance.

**Multi-attestor dilution.** The design requires no single attester to control a record: multiple independent attestations are structurally favored, which reference-check bias research recommends to dilute single-rater bias and which simultaneously raises the cost of retaliation (one bad actor cannot unilaterally tank a record) (Crosschq: https://www.crosschq.com/blog/reference-check-discrimination-how-to-ensure-fair-and-bias-free-hiring). Counsel may treat this as a background fact supporting the good-faith architecture.

**Philippines exposure.** Our research (`research/01-regulatory-perimeter.md` §3.9, flagged as its weakest section, secondary-sourced) records: no PH statute specifically prohibits private-sector blacklisting, but practitioner commentary treats cross-company do-not-hire lists as generally unlawful under the Data Privacy Act; false or malicious negative attestations expose employers and possibly the platform under Civil Code Arts. 19/20/21 and 26 and criminal libel (RPC Arts. 353–355; RA 10175 online aggravation); NPC Advisory Opinions 2018-050 and 2019-040 are cited in secondary sources as treating background verification for fraud-prevention/safety as legitimate interest with safeguards. **Counsel should pull these primary texts.** Separately note: data-privacy erasure exposure for adverse attestations (PH IRR Sec. 34(e)(e) "prejudicial information") is handled in Brief 1 and is not re-asked here, but the shield drafting should not contradict whatever remedy structure Brief 1 lands on.

**Timing fact.** The trust registry's state machine will not activate an external attesting party until this structure exists (design precondition, `design/ledger-design-0.1.md` §3.1). The founder has ruled the first external party is *not* confirmed within ~2 quarters, but the datacenter-technician contract is the canonical near-term case and partner conversations precede activation; the party agreement template must be final before the first external partner negotiation.

## 4. What the answer will drive, and when it is needed

The answer produces the **worker-agreement covenant/consent clauses and the external party agreement template**. These are the two instruments the first external attesting-party deal will be papered on. Decision points: (a) whether the shield is a covenant-not-to-sue, a consent-based privilege, statutory-immunity reliance, or a layered combination per state; (b) whether the dispute process becomes a contractual condition precedent to suit; (c) whether PH criminal-libel exposure requires structural product changes for PH-issued attestations (e.g., restricting negative free-text, coarser negative fields) rather than contract language alone, which would be a schema decision with long lead time. **Needed: before the first external attesting-party agreement is negotiated, with a target within two quarters.** The worker-agreement clauses also feed Brief 4's consent-instrument drafting and must land in the same drafting cycle.
