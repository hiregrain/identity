# Counsel engagement brief 5: worker-initiated verification, CRA status, EU lawful basis, controller allocation, and the roster firewall

**Jurisdictions:** United States (federal FCRA, California, Colorado, multi-state correction rules), European Union
**Date prepared:** 2026-08-19
**Status of this document:** Instructions to counsel. Internal research memos are cited throughout; they are not legal advice and have not been counsel-reviewed. Source URLs are provided so every proposition can be independently checked.

---

## 1. What the company and product are

The company (internally, "Grain") operates a **persistent worker identity and work-attestation ledger**. A worker holds a permanent platform identity (`ledger_person_id`, an opaque surrogate issued at universal self-signup with no proofing gate) and accumulates claims carrying an explicit provenance class: `self_asserted`, `peer_attested`, `party_attested`, with party attestations cryptographically signed (Ed25519/JWS) by registered attesting parties over a canonical payload and hash-chain timestamped. The record layer is free to every worker and every employer, permanently; the revenue product is **analytics on a person's work history and trajectory, sold to partner companies and used by internal verticals** (decision 022). Workers grant and revoke read access at the party level; partners receive a per-partner pairwise pseudonym, never the ledger identifier (decision 028). The ratified data contract is `model/attestation-interface.md` (schema_version 0.2, decision 006).

Two founder rulings fix the posture and are inputs, not open questions. **R1 (decision 003):** not a consumer reporting agency; the worker-owned platform model governs design, with the contrary internal FCRA analysis retained as evidence and counsel input. **R2 (decision 003):** whole-profile deletion at any time; verified records are never editable by the worker.

**The new mechanic this brief exists to test.** The founder has just ruled employment-history verification **worker-initiated**, replacing an earlier design in which employers pushed roster data about people who had not signed up. The replacement sequence is:

1. The worker creates a Grain identity themselves at universal self-signup.
2. The worker asserts a chapter of work history, such as employer, role, and dates, which enters the record as `self_asserted`.
3. The worker **requests verification** from that employer. The request is timestamped and carries a `verification_request_id`.
4. The employer confirms against its own roster, via a connected HR/staffing system or a human at the employer, and signs an attestation.
5. **The attestation enters the ledger. The employer's roster row never does.**

**The second plane, and the firewall.** Grain separately operates a **hospitality vertical**: a SaaS tool for restaurant and hotel operators. That vertical receives roster data from the employer's HR system and uses it for the employer's own staffing operations (openings opening and closing from team state). A firewall rule holds that **roster data serves the employer's operations only**; it never crosses into the ledger except through the worker-initiated path above, and it never feeds ranking or analytics for any party. We propose to enforce that boundary mechanically, not only contractually (§2 Q4, §3).

Grain today both holds the record and operates verticals on it; neutrality is maintained by contract, published rules and audit rather than corporate separation (decision 024). Counsel should hold that fact throughout, because it bears on Questions 3, 4 and 7.

## 2. Questions presented

1. **Does worker initiation defeat consumer-reporting-agency status in the United States?** The FTC's 2011 staff report *40 Years of Experience with the Fair Credit Reporting Act* contains, at comment 603(f)-4E, an exclusion for an intermediary acting on behalf of a consumer who initiated the transaction (https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/40-years-experience-fair-credit-reporting-act-ftc-staff-report-summary-interpretations/110720fcrareport.pdf). (a) Does the mechanic in §1 fall within that comment, and what weight does a staff-report interpretation carry against §1681a(f) in private litigation (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1681a)? (b) **What evidence of initiation must be retained, in what form, and for how long?** Is the timestamped `verification_request_id` plus the worker's authenticated session sufficient, or is a separate signed instrument per request required? (c) Does the answer change **when the employer connection is pre-existing**, meaning the employer is already a connected hospitality-vertical customer whose roster Grain already holds, **rather than established at the worker's request**? (d) Does the **analytics product sold to employers, computed on the record**, independently create CRA status regardless of how the record was assembled? And if so, is any version of it survivable under R1 (worker-directed delivery only, no employer-initiated pull, no ranking, coarse output, withheld below a confidence threshold)? Counsel should know that our own research assesses the **prior employer-push design as creating CRA status at high confidence**, element by element (`research/01-regulatory-perimeter.md` §1.1), and that the market appears to have sorted on exactly this axis: employer-pushed employment-verification platforms operate as CRAs, and the one platform we are aware of that claims non-CRA status is consumer-initiated. That characterisation is ours and should be tested against named comparables before it is relied on.

2. **Does the same initiation cure the EU lawful-basis problem?** Our research concluded there was **no lawful basis** for the employer-push design: consent failed twice over (sought from the wrong person, and unfree in the employment context, per Art. 29 WP Opinion 2/2017, `research/01` §2.1); Art. 6(1)(b) contract failed because the data subject was not party to the contract; and Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interests failed at the **necessity** limb under *KNLTB*, C-621/22 (https://curia.europa.eu/juris/liste.jsf?num=C-621/22), on the reasoning at ¶52–53 that a controller who could have asked the data subject first is not processing out of necessity. (a) Does worker initiation supply **Art. 6(1)(b) contract**, given the worker being party and verification being objectively necessary to deliver the service the worker asked for, or **Art. 6(1)(a) consent**, and which is preferable given Art. 7(3) withdrawal cascading into Art. 17(1)(b)? (b) What must the instrument say for the chosen basis to hold: does each verification request need its own just-in-time capture, and must the request itself name the recipient employer, the fields to be confirmed, and the append-only consequence? (c) Does initiation also cure the basis for the **employer's** side of the exchange, or does the employer need its own basis to confirm a former worker's dates to a third party?

3. **Controller allocation across the firewall.** Is Grain a **processor** for the employer as to roster data held for the employer's staffing operations, and an **independent controller** as to the ledger record? Or does operating both planes make it a **joint controller** throughout? Our research concluded that a "processor-until-claim" framing failed for the earlier design under EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 ¶40, ¶81 and ¶82 (https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-07/eppb_guidelines_202007_controllerprocessor_final_en.pdf), on the grounds that determining the purpose of a downstream product is a controller act however the data arrived. (a) Does the firewall change that conclusion, and does the answer turn on the mechanical separation in Question 4 or only on the contract? (b) What **contractual instruments** does counsel require, such as a DPA for the vertical plane, a controller-to-controller arrangement, or an Art. 26 joint-controller arrangement for the ledger plane, and how are they papered when both are signed by the same legal entity with the same customer? (c) Is there a configuration in which the same signed employer confirmation is simultaneously a processor output on one plane and a controller input on the other, and is that defensible or fatal?

4. **The firewall's sufficiency.** We propose to enforce the boundary mechanically: (i) **separate schemas** with no foreign key from any roster table to the ledger person identifier; (ii) a **required `verification_request_id`** on any roster-derived attestation, so no attestation can exist without a prior worker request; (iii) an **analytics input allowlist** that excludes roster tables by construction; (iv) a **cross-plane join ban**; (v) all four enforced in CI, so a violating migration or query fails the build rather than a review. (a) Does counsel regard mechanical enforcement plus contract as sufficient to sustain the answers to Questions 1–3, or is corporate or infrastructural separation required? (b) **What evidence of enforcement would counsel want retained for a regulator or a plaintiff?** Consider CI run artifacts, schema snapshots, a signed periodic attestation by an officer, or third-party audit, and for how long. (c) Is there any additional boundary counsel would add that we have not listed (identity resolution, support-tool access, shared operational staff, shared logs)?

5. **The negative and stale-roster cases.** A worker asserts employment at a connected employer and the roster returns **no match**, commonly because the employer connected to the vertical *after* the worker left, so the roster never contained them. Our person-matching research is directly relevant: probabilistic record linkage produces false negatives from name variants, transliteration and channel changes, and weak-field matching at scale produces a persistent harm class (`research/03-person-id-and-merge.md` §2). (a) What may Grain **record** and what may it **display** in that state? (b) Does a stated non-confirmation ("employer did not confirm") create defamation exposure, or FCRA/ICRAA accuracy exposure under §1681e(b) if Question 1 resolves against us? (c) **Is silence safer than a stated negative?** And if silence is chosen, does the resulting asymmetry (verified claims marked, unverified claims merely unmarked) itself become an accuracy or fairness problem when the record is read by employers? (d) Is there a defensible third state, such as "not found in this employer's connected system; this employer connected on [date]," that discloses the mechanism without asserting a fact about the worker?

6. **Colorado's correction rule against founder ruling R2.** Colorado's CPA Rules, 4 CCR 904-3 Rule 4.06(D)(4), provide that where a controller did **not** receive personal data directly from the consumer and holds **no documentation supporting its accuracy**, the consumer's assertion of inaccuracy is **sufficient to establish** that the data is inaccurate (https://coag.gov/app/uploads/2023/03/FINAL-CLEAN-2023.03.15-Official-CPA-Rules.pdf). R2 holds that a worker may never edit a verified record, though they may delete their whole profile at any time; decision 028 provides a **dispute-annotation** path, under which the claim stays visible and marked, with the worker's statement attached, never silently removed, and rules that Grain adjudicates authenticity, never substance. (a) Does annotation discharge Rule 4.06(D)(4), or does the rule require deletion or correction of the item? (b) Does the signed employer attestation itself constitute "documentation supporting accuracy" sufficient to take the item outside the rule? And does the answer differ for a `self_asserted` chapter the worker authored (received directly from the consumer, and in any event editable per decision 028) versus a `party_attested` one? (c) **Which other states have equivalents**, and do any go further? (d) What is the **minimum compliant design**, meaning the least departure from R2 that satisfies the strictest state? Counsel should note that if Question 1 resolves against R1, §1681i(a)(5)(A) independently requires deletion or modification of inaccurate, incomplete or unverifiable items, and annotation-only is non-compliant (`research/01` §1.5).

7. **Grain as attesting party about workers in its own verticals.** The hospitality vertical's operators are Grain's customers, but Grain's own internal verticals also employ and attest about workers, and organisations are never subjects, so a customer organisation registers as an attesting party and writes back (decision 007). (a) Where the confirming employer *is* Grain or a Grain vertical, does the "intermediary acting on behalf of the consumer" framing in Question 1 survive, or does Grain become the maker of a report about its own experience that is then furnished to others, which is the point at which §1681a(d)(2)(A)(i) breaks on our own analysis (`research/01` §1.2)? (b) Is the affiliate lane at §1681a(d)(2)(A)(ii)–(iii) available between internal verticals and the ledger, and what disclosure and opt-out would it require? (c) Does the same-entity relationship contaminate the controller allocation in Question 3?

## 3. Relevant facts and our current working analysis

Counsel should treat everything below as our position to be confirmed or corrected.

**The prior design and why it was abandoned.** The employer-push design had employers transmitting roster data about people who had never signed up, with a claim raised later. Our research assessed that design as satisfying §1681a(f) element by element: fees from partners (the worker being free is irrelevant), "regularly engages… in whole or in part", assembling and evaluating, for the purpose of furnishing to third parties, and the prior packet as a consumer report under §1681a(d)(1) used for "employment purposes" as defined at §1681a(h), which expressly reaches promotion, reassignment and retention of *existing* employees (`research/01` §1.1; https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1681a). Self-labelling is irrelevant (FTC 2013 guidance, https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2013/01/background-screening-reports-fcra-just-saying-youre-not-consumer-reporting-agency-isnt-enough; *Spokeo*, https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2012/06/spokeo-pay-800000-settle-ftc-charges-company-allegedly-marketed-information-employers-recruiters), and *Kidd v. Thomson Reuters Corp.*, 925 F.3d 99 (2d Cir. 2019)'s intent-based test is one our product satisfies by design and by pitch rather than escapes (https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/17-3550/17-3550-2019-05-30.html). **Confidence: high, and the memo is on record, which matters because a written internal conclusion later rejected is §1681n willfulness evidence.** The worker-initiated mechanic is the first design change that goes to the *assembly* element rather than to consent, visibility or permissioning, all three of which our research found *strengthen* the CRA case because each maps onto an existing CRA duty (§1681b(a)(2), §1681g, §1681e(a); `research/01` §1.4). That is precisely why Question 1 is asked as a fresh question and not as a re-run of Brief 4's Question 5.

**Why the analytics sub-question (1d) may be dispositive on its own.** Analytics is the first product and ships from day one (decision 022; THESIS §5). It is computed on the record, delivered to a partner, not retained as a fact about the person; run records hold the run and nothing attributable to a person (decision 026). Mitigations that are real: no derived score in any fact table, coarse and citation-backed outputs, every mark resolving to its evidence, and withholding rather than softening below a confidence threshold. Against that: the withdrawn CFPB Circular 2024-06 addressed this exact fact pattern and stated an entity could "assemble" or "evaluate" by collecting consumer data to train an algorithm producing scores about workers for employers; we treat the withdrawal as a change in enforcement probability, not in legal exposure (https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/circulars/consumer-financial-protection-circular-2024-06-background-dossiers-and-algorithmic-scores-for-hiring-promotion-and-other-employment-decisions/; withdrawal: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/05/12/2025-08286/interpretive-rules-policy-statements-and-advisory-opinions-withdrawal). **THESIS §7 states the position plainly: selling capability projections to employers moves R1 from theoretical to load-bearing.** One product fact counsel should weigh in the balance rather than discover: **workers do not see when analytics are run on them** (decision 022, recorded as a deliberate cost), which is the inverse of §1681g(a)(3)(A)(i)'s two-year recipient disclosure.

**California, and why federal is not the binding constraint.** ICRAA (Cal. Civ. Code §1786 et seq.) reaches investigative consumer reports obtained "through any means," without the federal personal-interview limitation; *Connor v. First Student, Inc.*, 5 Cal.5th 1026 (2018) held overlap with the CCRAA does not excuse compliance (https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-supreme-court/1948536.html); statutory damages are $10,000 per violation. Counsel should answer Question 1 for ICRAA separately, not by reference to the federal answer.

**EU.** The lawful-basis analysis in `research/01` §2.1 predates the worker-initiated mechanic and was written against employer push. Art. 6(1)(f) remains our expected basis for *maintaining* verified history; the question here is narrower and prior: whether the **acquisition** of a confirmation about a named worker now rests on contract or consent because the worker asked for it. Note the interaction with R2 and with Art. 16: decision 028 already records that Art. 16 rectification exists regardless of the founder's no-countersign ruling, so a correction path must exist as a post-hoc dispute. Note also, without asking for an opinion here, that the analytics layer is high-risk under EU AI Act Annex III point 4 with the Art. 6(3) derogation unavailable because profiling forecloses it (THESIS §7); Grain's ruled posture is to build with the constraints in mind and not to build for the EU market first (decision 024).

**The firewall as designed, in engineering terms.** The ledger already runs a two-plane split with mechanical checks in CI, including a ban on capability-score columns in fact tables (`plans/foundation/06-checks-port.md`) and a spine-linkage threat model that explicitly contemplates joining commitments against a known external roster as an attack (`plans/foundation/09-spine-linkage-threat-model.md`). The four firewall rules in Question 4 are proposed as additions in the same enforcement style. The ratified interface already forbids shadow records in both directions and states that the ledger holds attestations about work, never the work itself (`model/attestation-interface.md`). **One tension counsel should see rather than infer:** the interface states that "a person without a ledger ID cannot be attested for; the attestation waits until the reference exists," which describes a queue of employer-originated attestations awaiting a subject, a shape the worker-initiated mechanic is meant to eliminate. We read the new ruling as superseding that sentence; if counsel's answer to Question 1 depends on no such queue existing, say so explicitly and we will amend the contract.

**Matching and the negative case.** Roster confirmation is a record-linkage operation. Our research (`research/03`) concluded that no credible large-scale system merges on a single weak anchor, that phone and email are contact anchors rather than identity anchors (recycling windows, SIM swap, shared household handsets in Philippine and other markets), and that weak-field matching at scale produced the credit bureaus' "mixed file" harm class. A roster non-match is therefore evidence about a matching process, not about a worker, which is the substance of Question 5.

**Colorado, and what is already ruled.** Decision 028 rules that Grain adjudicates authenticity, never substance, expressly *because* FCRA §1681i reinvestigation is a defining CRA duty and performing it voluntarily cuts against R1 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1681i). Rule 4.06(D)(4) pushes the opposite way for the non-CRA branch, and the two pressures meet on the same item. Decision 028 also softened R2 at the edges: self-asserted work history admits "minor edits," and removal requires contacting support with a justification. So the class of records to which Rule 4.06(D)(4) bites hardest is precisely the `party_attested` class the founder ruled frozen.

## 4. What the answer will drive, and when it is needed

The mechanic is **pre-implementation**. Nothing here is a retrofit request; all of it is cheap now.

- **Question 1 determines whether the FCRA compliance apparatus must be built at all**: reinvestigation tooling, obsolescence time-scoping, adverse-action artifacts, furnisher dispute-routing callbacks to every attesting party, reader certifications at partner onboarding. That is the single largest conditional build in the plan tree. Question 1(d) additionally determines whether the analytics product ships in its designed form; since the record layer is free forever, analytics carries all the revenue. **Needed first, and before the verification flow is built.**
- **Question 2 gates EU launch.** No EU worker is onboarded before it is answered.
- **Questions 3 and 4 determine the database architecture**: schema separation, key structure, and the CI check set. Expensive to change later, cheap now. **Needed before the two-plane migration lands.**
- **Question 5 blocks the verification flow shipping.** The flow cannot be built without knowing what the non-match state renders.
- **Question 6 may force a change to a ratified founder decision (R2, decision 003, as implemented by decision 028).** If annotation does not discharge the rule, the founder must choose between a correction path and a jurisdictional scoping of R2. That decision belongs to the founder and needs counsel's answer to be made properly.
- **Question 7** conditions how internal verticals are papered against the ledger and whether the affiliate lane is worth its disclosure cost.

## 5. What we are explicitly not asking

- **Not** data residency, cross-border transfer, or erasure-architecture sufficiency. See Brief 1.
- **Not** the salted-commitment or public transparency-log posture. See Brief 2.
- **Not** attesting-party liability, defamation strategy, or the covenant-not-to-sue architecture. See Brief 3. Question 5(b) asks only whether a *stated non-confirmation* is a distinct exposure; the general shield analysis stays there.
- **Not** worker-agreement or consent-instrument drafting. See Brief 4. Questions 1(b) and 2(b) ask what the instrument must *capture and say*; the drafting lands in Brief 4's cycle.
- **Not** labour-market information exchange or antitrust exposure of a cross-employer performance register under the January 2025 DOJ/FTC guidelines. See Brief 6 (unscoped; THESIS §9 records it as outside every frame used so far).
- **Not** EU AI Act conformity, Art. 22 / automated-decision exposure, or the Colorado SB 26-189 developer duties. These are separately scoped.
- **Not** KYC/AML, sanctions, or right-to-work perimeter questions.

**One overlap counsel must resolve rather than route around.** Brief 3 and Brief 4 have been found to overlap on **15 U.S.C. §1681h(e)** (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1681h), whose qualified immunity from state defamation and invasion-of-privacy claims, absent malice or wilful intent to injure, runs **only to consumer reporting agencies, users of consumer reports, and furnishers to CRAs**. **The answer to Question 1 therefore determines whether Brief 3's contractual liability-shield architecture is necessary at all**, or whether some of it is available by statute at the cost of the status R1 exists to avoid. Counsel should state that dependency explicitly in the Question 1 answer rather than leaving the three briefs to be reconciled by us.
